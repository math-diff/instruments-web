Industrial temperature points answer two questions: **what does the
operator see in the field**, and **what does the control system
receive**. Different instruments serve each purpose — bimetal
thermometers for local indication, RTDs (Pt100) or thermocouples for
signal transmission. This guide explains the division of labor and
the selection essentials.

## 1. Who does what

| Instrument | Role | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bimetal thermometer | Local indication, no power needed | Pumps, tanks, piping, bearing housings |
| RTD (Pt100 etc.) | Transmitted signal, high accuracy at low-to-mid temperatures | Reactors, thermal-oil heaters, bearings and windings |
| Thermocouple (TC) | Transmitted signal, high-temperature service | Furnaces, power-plant boilers, hot chemical lines |

A **dual installation** at the same point — one bimetal thermometer
for the field plus one RTD for the DCS — is the most common engineering
configuration: the control room gets the signal, operators get a
local reading, and each checks the other.

## 2. Bimetal thermometers: construction and selection

A bimetal thermometer winds two metal strips with different expansion
coefficients into a helix; temperature change rotates the helix and
drives the pointer directly. No power supply, mechanically rugged —
a pure field instrument.

Selection points:

- **Range**: normal operating temperature between one-third and
  two-thirds of span. Common spans: 0–150 °C, 0–300 °C, 0–500 °C.
- **Accuracy class**: classes 1.5 and 2.5 are typical; 2.5 is
  generally sufficient for local monitoring.
- **Stem diameter and insertion depth**: effective insertion depth
  should be ≥ 15–20 stem diameters to limit conduction error;
  sheath in 304/316L stainless per the media.
- **Connection**: threaded (M27×2 common) for direct pipe insertion;
  union-nut versions allow periodic withdrawal for calibration.
- **Dial orientation**: axial (pointer parallel to stem) for panel
  mounting; radial (pointer perpendicular to stem) for vertical pipe
  installation facing the walkway.
- **Vibration**: near pumps and rotating machinery, specify the
  **liquid-filled (damped) type** — an undamped pointer will blur.

## 3. RTD or thermocouple: decide by temperature, then accuracy

**Step 1: temperature range**

| Range | First choice |
| --- | --- |
| −50 °C to +400 °C | RTD (Pt100) |
| +400 °C to +850 °C | RTD upper limit or Type K thermocouple |
| Above +850 °C | Thermocouple (Type K continuous to ~1000 °C, Type S to ~1300 °C) |

**Step 2: within the same range, compare accuracy and stability**

- The Pt100 RTD is **more accurate** in the −50 to 400 °C band,
  more stable, and needs no cold junction — the default choice for
  low and mid temperatures. A Class A Pt100 tolerates
  ±(0.15 + 0.002·|t|) °C (per IEC 60751; Class A applies from about
  −30 to 350 °C) — i.e. ±0.15 °C at 0 °C and ±0.85 °C at 350 °C.
- Thermocouples are simple and tolerate heat and shock, but are less
  accurate (Type K Class 1 per IEC 60584: ±1.5 °C from −40 to 375 °C,
  ±0.004·|t| above), need cold-junction compensation, and their
  millivolt signals are more noise-prone.

**Step 3: wiring**

RTDs come in two-, three- and four-wire versions. **Three-wire is the
industrial standard** — the lead resistance is cancelled by measuring
through two arms of the bridge. Long leads without three-wire
compensation add lead resistance directly as measurement error.
Four-wire is for laboratory-grade accuracy.

## 4. Installation and common problems

- **Insertion**: against the flow or perpendicular to it, with the
  measuring tip near the pipe centerline.
- **Thermowells**: high-velocity dusty flows (coal milling, powder
  conveying) need **wear-resistant** elements with reinforced tips;
  plain wells erode quickly.
- **Hazardous areas**: use flameproof junction boxes (Ex d) selected
  and installed per GB/T 3836 (IEC 60079 series).
- **Typical faults**: jumping readings point to loose terminals or
  mixing compensation cable with ordinary cable; a consistent offset
  points to insufficient insertion depth or sheath fouling.
